You finish the book. You agree with all of it. You open your editor, you type `mkdir src/`, and then you sit there.

The rings told you which way a dependency points. They did not tell you what to call a single folder, and the gap between those two things is where you will spend the next three years.

Somebody noticed that gap, and the way they handled it is the most interesting thing about the book.

Clean Architecture spends thirty-three chapters on rings, boundaries and dependencies. Chapter 34 is about what you type into `mkdir`, and Robert Martin did not write it. Simon Brown did, the author of the C4 model, invited into another author's book to point out that no amount of circles tells you what to call the folder. He called his contribution The Missing Chapter, which is a quietly pointed thing to call your own section of somebody else's book.

His argument is that the devil is in the implementation details, and that it is easy to fall at the last hurdle if you never give them any thought. Thirty-three chapters of theory, and then somebody has to put the files somewhere.

What he puts in front of you is four ways to group the same code. The units never change between them. Only the boundary somebody drew around those units changes.

**Package by layer** slices horizontally. Every controller together, every service together, every repository together. It is what most frameworks hand you on day one, and it means a single change to one capability touches three directories that have nothing else to do with each other.

**Package by feature** slices vertically. Everything one capability needs, in one place.

**Ports and adapters** does not slice at all. It draws a core and puts adapters around it, which is the previous chapter's drawing with folder names on it.

**Package by component** is feature slicing with one extra rule: each slice has exactly one unit that anybody outside is allowed to call. The rest is yours.

The same four, as directories you could type. Step through them and the file names stay put while the folders around them move.

Put that way the four look like a matter of taste, and they are not. Layering optimises for somebody who knows what kind of thing they are looking for. Feature and component slicing optimise for somebody who knows what the software does. In my experience the second person shows up far more often, and the first one is usually just new.

The one I keep landing on is the last, and not because vertical slices are fashionable. It is the extra rule. A slice with no declared entrance is a slice that will be entered from anywhere, and then it is not a slice any more. It took me a few projects to work out that a boundary is worth nothing unless something enforces where the door is.

Which brings me to what that looks like in a tree you can actually clone.

The boilerplate this series is built on puts each bounded context at the root of `src/`, with `Application/`, `Domain/` and `Infrastructure/` inside it, and `Ui/` sitting beside the contexts rather than inside any of them. That last decision is the subject of several chapters further on, and I am not going to argue it here.

What is worth saying now is what is not in that tree. `src/` holds two directories, `Shared/` and `Ui/`. There is no `src/<Context>/` at all, and that is on purpose. A boilerplate can ship you the machinery, the architecture rules and the arguments for both. It cannot ship you a bounded context, because which contexts exist is the one decision that is entirely about your business and not at all about software. The day somebody hands you a starter kit with `Billing/` already in it, they have made the only decision you could not delegate.

So the tree in that repository is a shape with a hole in the middle, and the hole is the point.

The book that argues hardest about dependency direction needed a guest author to say where the files go, and he opened by telling you this is the hurdle people fall at. The tree is not a matter of taste. It is where you spend the rule from last time, and the four groupings above are four ways of spending it.
