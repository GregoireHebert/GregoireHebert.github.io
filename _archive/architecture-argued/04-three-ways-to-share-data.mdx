Last time ended on a condition and no mechanism. You may ask your neighbour for a value, as long as that value carries no meaning in the domain you are standing in. Now for how it crosses.

Most people will tell you there are two patterns here and you pick one: publish domain events, or keep a local read model of what the neighbour knows. Count them properly and there are three. Look at two of them closely and there is one.

**Domain events.** A context publishes when data the business cares about changes. The contexts that listen write what they need into their own store. Synchronous or asynchronous is a transport decision and nothing more.

**The local data mechanism.** A context keeps a read model of what it needs from its neighbour, fed by that neighbour's publications. Asynchronous means eventual consistency, and you say that out loud on the day you choose it rather than discovering it in an incident.

**The open host service.** The blue book's name for a protocol you open so that everyone who needs you can integrate. The call belongs in the Application or UI layer. A domain that depends on an external service has given up its separation of concerns, and a client that makes that call has walked off with the business logic in its pocket.

Say eventual consistency out loud and somebody will wince, which is unfair to a phrase that had to be argued for in public.

In October 2008, Werner Vogels published Eventually Consistent in ACM Queue. He was Amazon's CTO, writing about S3, SimpleDB and Dynamo, and the essay's actual argument is that a system at that scale has to trade consistency against availability, and that a storage system which guarantees you will eventually see the last written value is a reasonable thing to build on. A company that size needed its CTO to write that down and publish it, because the default assumption was that data is correct now or the system is broken.

The phrase did its job so well that people now reach for it as a design rather than as a consequence. It is a consequence.

The first two panels are the same picture. That is not a mistake in the drawing.

A publishes, B listens, B writes its own store. Identical wire, identical code, identical failure modes. What differs is what you meant.

The first time you meant a chain reaction. An order is created, so an invoice is created, so an accounting record is written. Each step is a business consequence of the one before it, and somebody could read the sequence aloud in a meeting and it would make sense to a person who has never seen the code.

The second time you meant replication. B needs to answer a question quickly, or without A being up, so B keeps its own copy. There is no business story. There is a performance and availability story.

Same mechanism, two intentions, and the wire carries no trace of which one you had in mind. Six months later somebody reads your event handler and has to guess, and the guess decides whether they feel free to change it.

So write it down. Not in a comment next to the handler, which goes stale the day the handler moves, but in whatever place your project keeps decisions.

Three things to avoid, while we are here. A direct query into a neighbour's tables marries you to their schema, which is the previous chapter's mistake with a database driver attached. A synchronous call over a bus trades availability for convenience, and you will not notice the trade until the neighbour is slow rather than down. And calling the neighbour's services directly when both contexts live in one source tree throws the boundary away while the directories still suggest it is there, which is the worst of the three because it looks fine in review.
